Figurative vs Literal Meaning

An Idiom is a fixed figurative expression whose collective meaning cannot be deduced simply from the literal definitions of its individual words.

The Cardinal Rule of Idioms:
• The correct answer in exams is almost NEVER the literal interpretation of the words used.
• e.g., "Bite the bullet" has nothing to do with ammunition; it means facing a grim situation with courage.

High-Frequency Idiom Categories

⚔️ Conflict & Decision
At daggers drawn | Cross swords

🎯 Accuracy & Success
Hit the nail on the head | Come with flying colors
State precisely the truth | Achieve outstanding triumph.
🌩️ Risk & Danger
Skate on thin ice | Hanging by a thread
Take massive risks | In a precarious or extremely vulnerable state.
🤫 Secrecy & Deception
Spill the beans | Wolf in sheep's clothing

Step-by-Step Solved Examples

Ex 1 Identify the meaning of the idiom: "To leave no stone unturned"
Options: (A) To search for precious gems  |  (B) To try every possible means to achieve something  |  (C) To build a solid foundation  |  (D) To damage public property
Origin / Metaphor Historical legend of searching every corner and overturning stones for hidden treasure.
Literal Elimination Options (A) and (D) rely on physical stones (literal trap → eliminate).
Solution Correct figurative meaning is Option B: To try every possible means.
✓ Final Answer: To try every possible means
Ex 2 Choose the correct idiom for: "To face an impending disaster with calmness and without panic"
Options: (A) Burn the candle at both ends  |  (B) Keep one's head  |  (C) Play fast and loose  |  (D) Cry over spilt milk
Definition Check Target attribute: Emotional composure and presence of mind under severe crisis.
Idiom Mapping "To keep one's head" = maintain calm in distress. ("To lose one's head" = panic).
Solution Correct match is Option B: Keep one's head.
✓ Final Answer: Keep one's head

Speed-Solving Tips & Shortcuts

🚫
Immediate Literal Elimination: Any option that references the concrete physical nouns of the idiom (e.g. actual dogs in "barking dogs", real cats in "raining cats and dogs") is almost certainly wrong.
🧠
Root Story Association: Associate idioms with their origins (e.g. Achilles' heel comes from Greek mythology where the heel was the only vulnerable spot).
⚡
Tone Polarity: Categorize idioms as inherently Positive (e.g. feather in one's cap) or Negative (e.g. wild-goose chase) to eliminate opposing options.
🔍
Contextual Sentence Clues: When tested inside a sentence, substitute the phrase with your plain English guess to verify compatibility.
⚠️

Common Mistakes to Avoid

🚫
Mistake 1: Falling for the Literal Interpretation Trap
Examiners deliberately craft multiple-choice options with literal word-for-word descriptions to mislead unprepared candidates.
🚫
Mistake 2: Confusing Similar Sounding Idioms
Be precise with distinct idioms: "Burn the candle at both ends" (overwork) vs "Burn the midnight oil" (study late into the night).
🚫
Mistake 3: Altering Fixed Prepositions in Idioms
Idioms have fixed syntax. "In the nick of time" is correct; "at the nick of time" is grammatically erroneous in sentence improvement.
